Read-Only, On Purpose

Brent Blackmon

A post crossed my feed this week from Andrea Masnata, a finance executive who works on growth and profitability for scaling technology companies. Her opening line: she can finally talk to her ERP using Claude.

That's not a product announcement. It's a field report, and it happens to retrace four separate arguments this series has made, one after another, inside a single company's build.

The vendor still hasn't shipped it

She's specific about why she had to build the natural-language layer herself. The ERP vendor claims to have embedded AI, and in practice it doesn't work, so her team figured out the backend on their own. That's the same gap I wrote about with TIAA and Accenture in the fourth essay in this series, and the same one that got InsightsAI, Data Rocket, and Code Rocket built in the first place. The vendor doesn't ship the accelerator. You build it because you're the one who has to hit the deadline.

The questions came first, the framework came after

She started where InsightsAI started: asking the ERP a plain question in plain language, largest suppliers last month, bank balances over six months, instead of navigating a report writer built for someone else's job. Spotting an anomaly stopped requiring a specialist. That's the access problem I wrote about in the first essay in this series, solved the same way in a completely different company.

She didn't stop there. Once she'd spent enough time in the data to trust what she was looking at, she turned it into an actual control framework, a running list of checks that fire on demand or on their own. Data quality stops waiting on whatever the human review cadence happens to be that quarter. That's the same discipline I described chairing the AI Council three essays back: every initiative accountable to what data it touched and how a result could be traced back and explained. She built the informal version of that on her own, without a council, because the standard doesn't change based on who's enforcing it.

Read-only, on purpose

She's explicit about where she draws the line right now. Letting the model write to the ERP, in her words, "does not seem like a career-enhancing move," not yet, not at the current state of the technology. It stays read-only.

That's the human-in-the-loop discipline I built into Otto from day one, and the fourth trait I listed back in the third essay of this series: human-controlled, not autonomous by default. Automation earns write access in phases. It doesn't get handed the keys because the read layer worked.

Where this leaves the argument

Last essay was about what happens when a fleet of agents runs on top of a foundation, trustworthy or not. Masnata's post is the same argument scaled down to one person and one ERP: build the access yourself when the vendor won't, turn ad hoc questions into a standing discipline once you trust the answers, and don't hand over the keys just because the read layer works. Four threads, from four separate essays in this series, all showing up in one person's actual project.

The AI layer is the amplifier. The order you do things in, build the access, earn the trust, then decide what gets automated, is still the point. That's been true across all eight of these essays now, whether it's a trillion-dollar asset manager, a mid-market manufacturer, or one finance executive with a Claude window open next to her ERP.
