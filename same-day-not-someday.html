Same Day, Not Someday

Brent Blackmon

Two posts crossed my feed this week that show exactly what happens once the tracks under an operation are actually fixed. Neither one is about a model release. Both are about work getting done differently, today, because the workflow underneath finally lets it.

Same-day solutioning is now the baseline

The CEO of a small ERP reporting software company posted about a customer forum request that came in four hours before he wrote about it. A customer flagged a real gap in one of their Excel functions, one that made users export a full list and eyeball it by hand instead of filtering it directly. By the time he posted, there was already a workaround live and a preview of the permanent fix. His line: "while others ship twice per year, we ship twice before lunch."

That's not an AI story on its face. It's a workflow story, a small team, no product committee, no six-month release train, a direct line from the customer's complaint to a shipped fix. But it's the same pattern I've been describing across this whole series: the companies compounding value right now are the ones who removed the queue between a problem surfacing and someone acting on it. AI accelerates that loop once it exists. It doesn't create the loop by itself.

Agents are running the job, not just answering the question

The second post comes from someone further down the same road. He's stopped treating AI as a tool he opens for an answer and started running it like a team: a named agent for code, one for marketing, one for drafting documents, one for sales activity, two personal assistants. He messages them the way he'd message a person on Slack, and the work comes back finished instead of sitting in his inbox as a task he still owes.

The operating discipline behind it matches almost exactly what I've argued for two years running AI inside an ERP company: one job per agent, not one assistant trying to do everything, clean context per channel, and a person still deciding what ships. His shorthand for the model, plan, orchestrate, validate, is as good a description of where this is headed as I've seen.

The part that should change how you think about headcount

He put the real implication plainly: for years the question was which software your team should use. Now you can staff whole roles with agents and review their output. That's not a productivity-tool decision anymore. It belongs in the same conversation as who you hire, not buried in a tools budget.

I've been living a smaller version of this for two years. Otto Mayshun and InsightsAI started as customer-facing products, but the more interesting shift internally was standing up agents that took over actual functions: one surfacing sales leads, one running marketing operations, one drafting statements of work, one flagging P&L anomalies before month-end close. None of them is a chatbot bolted onto a workflow. Each one owns a piece of the operation the way a person would, on a narrow mandate, with a human still reviewing what comes out the other end.

Where this leaves the argument

Last essay I said the AI layer is the amplifier and the data and workflow underneath are still the point. These two posts are what the amplifier looks like once the sequencing is right: solutioning that used to take a release cycle now takes an afternoon, and roles that used to require a hire now run as a reviewed agent with a narrow job. Neither one is available to a company that skipped the rewiring. Both are available, right now, to one that didn't.
