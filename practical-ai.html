What Practical AI Actually Looks Like

Brent Blackmon

Skip the argument about whether AI is transformational or overhyped. Neither camp is much help to a 250-person small or medium business trying to decide what to do this quarter.

Most of the public debate about AI sits at one of two extremes: it's inevitable and transformational, or it's mostly hype riding a market cycle. Both camps are talking past the people who actually have to make a decision this quarter, run operations on a system implemented a decade ago, with a team that doesn't have time for either the hype or the skepticism.

Here's what I've learned actually works, from building and rolling out AI tools on top of ERP systems that were never designed with AI in mind.

Start with the data you have

The biggest myth slowing companies down is that you need clean data before you can start. In practice, the opposite works better: deploy an analytics layer on top of what you already have, and let it show you what needs cleaning. Waiting twelve to eighteen months for a perfect data foundation before doing anything useful is how most of these initiatives die before they ship. Start with the data you have, and let it show you what to clean.

Four things that separate practical AI from the rest

Not every AI tool aimed at operations is worth deploying. The ones that actually hold up share four traits.

It's built for the system, not bolted onto it. A generic API connection gives you generic answers. A tool that actually understands your ERP's schema and workflows gives you answers that match operational reality.

Pricing you can forecast. Token-based billing might be fine for a hobby project. For a business trying to budget, unpredictable usage costs are a non-starter.

Delivered by people who know your system. A tool implemented by someone who understands your specific configuration outperforms a generic rollout every time.

Human-controlled, not autonomous by default. The goal is decision support, faster answers with a person still deciding what to do about them, not an agent quietly taking action nobody signed off on.

What a realistic timeline looks like

An initial conversation, about 30 minutes, focused on business priorities rather than technology. Environment setup in one to two weeks, using pre-built dashboards rather than starting from a blank page. Live analytics running by week three or four, with anomaly detection following shortly after. Somewhere around 90 days, the team stops pulling data and starts analyzing why the data looks the way it does. That's the inflection point, and it's the moment the value starts compounding instead of just accumulating.

None of this is exotic. It's disciplined sequencing applied to a system most companies already own. The organizations that get it right aren't the ones with the most advanced AI. They're the ones who treated the rollout like an operations project instead of a technology purchase.
