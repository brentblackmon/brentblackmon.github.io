The Cost of Standing Still

Brent Blackmon

Most companies using AI aren't seeing a return on it. The ones that are didn't start with AI. They started with visibility, and let AI compound on top of it.

A common, reasonable instinct in most executive teams right now is to wait. Wait for the AI landscape to settle, wait for a clearer winner among the vendors, wait until the risk of picking wrong goes down. It's a defensible instinct. Some of that waiting is smart.

But some of it is expensive, and the expense is easy to miss because it never shows up as a line item. It shows up as decisions made a month later than they should have been, over and over, for years.

The gap between adoption and return

Roughly 88% of companies have adopted some form of AI. By most accounts, only about 6% report a genuine financial return from it. That's not an AI problem. It's a sequencing problem. The organizations seeing a return built visibility into how decisions get made before they layered AI on top. The other 82% bought the tool first and are still waiting for the workflow, the trust, and the data underneath it to catch up.

For a small or medium business, that gap doesn't announce itself. It shows up as a finance leader who spots margin erosion after the month is already closed instead of before it. An operations manager who has to request a report and wait instead of getting an answer on the spot. A CEO walking into a board meeting having to ask for numbers that should already be in front of them.

Why the hesitation is rational, and where it goes wrong

In a survey of more than 200 operations leaders, 26% said they're planning AI investment despite rating their own readiness at an average of 4 out of 10, and nearly half put themselves at 3 or lower. The barriers they named weren't exotic: no clear starting point, doubt about whether their data could be trusted, and scar tissue from a past project that didn't work.

That hesitation is rational. What's not rational is treating it as a reason to do nothing rather than a reason to start smaller and closer to the data you already have. AI that just answers a question without flagging what it doesn't trust in the underlying data is worse than no AI at all, it produces confident wrong answers faster than a person would have gotten to a wrong answer on their own. The fix isn't waiting longer. It's picking a rollout that surfaces the data gaps as it goes instead of assuming they don't exist.

What standing still actually costs

The cost compounds in three places: margin opportunities missed because nobody saw them in time, small delivery problems that turn into customer complaints because nobody caught them early, and leadership meetings spent debating whose numbers are right instead of what to do about them. None of that shows up on a P&L as "cost of not adopting AI." It shows up as a slightly worse quarter, quietly, for years in a row.

The way out isn't a big bang AI initiative. It's building on the ERP foundation that's already there, in a way that shows you both the answer and how much to trust it.
