The Tracks Are the Constraint

Brent Blackmon

Fortune ran a piece this week from leaders at TIAA and Accenture with a line that belongs on the wall of every AI budget meeting in the country: a company can buy the fastest trains in the world and it still won't matter if it keeps running them on the same aging rails. The tracks are the constraint, not the trains.

That's an enterprise argument, made about a company managing more than a trillion dollars in assets. But it's the exact argument I've been making about small and medium businesses for three essays now, and seeing it show up at that scale, with that much money behind it, is worth stopping on.

The rewiring, not the plugin

The numbers in that piece should worry anyone treating AI as a bolt-on. Only about 5% of businesses say their data is actually AI-ready. Gartner expects roughly 60% of AI projects to be abandoned through the end of this year for the same underlying reason, not because the model was wrong, but because nothing underneath it was ready to be reasoned over. That's not a model problem. It's an operating-model problem wearing an AI costume.

TIAA's answer wasn't a faster model. It was modernizing the digital infrastructure first, then layering AI on top of workflows that had already been redesigned to use it. The result: 85% daily adoption of their generative platform among employees, plan-option changes that used to take weeks now taking days, and digital engagement up 13% across participants. None of that came from a smarter chatbot. It came from fixing the tracks before running trains on them.

"AI transformation isn't a technology project. It's a business transformation, a change-management project, and an operating-model rebuild that happens to run on AI."

What this looks like below the enterprise

I most recently ran strategy for a company with a fraction of TIAA's resources and none of its budget for a multi-year Accenture engagement, and I'd argue the sequencing problem is identical, just smaller and faster to fix. A 250-person manufacturer on a decade-old ERP doesn't need a rewiring project measured in years. It needs the same three things TIAA needed, scaled down: infrastructure that can actually serve data to an AI layer, workflows redesigned around getting an answer instead of requesting one, and a rollout that treats trust in the data as the deliverable, not an afterthought.

The advantage a smaller company has here is real, and it's one most of the AI vendor pitches skip past. TIAA had to rewire decades of systems built by and for a trillion-dollar institution. A mid-market business usually has one system of record, not twelve. The rewiring job is smaller because the railroad is smaller. That's the whole argument from the first essay in this series, and it's the reason I don't think the SMB opportunity here is a scaled-down version of the enterprise one. In some ways it's the easier version of the same problem.

That advantage is worth protecting. A recent CIO.com roundup of ERP trends reports enterprises moving off monolithic ERP toward unbundled, best-of-breed stacks across finance, HR, procurement, and supply chain, snapped together like Lego. With an enterprise integration team behind it, that can work. For a 250-person manufacturer, it's a way to buy back the stitching problem it never had, and hand the AI layer six partial records to reason over instead of one.

The sequencing question, one more time

Every company I talk to already knows, at some level, that the model isn't the hard part anymore. What TIAA's case makes harder to ignore is that skipping the rewiring doesn't just slow the AI rollout down, it caps how much of the investment ever shows up as a return, no matter how good the model gets next quarter. The 60% of projects Gartner expects to get abandoned this year aren't failing because the labs stopped shipping. They're failing because nobody rewired the tracks first.

Deloitte is telling its ERP clients the same thing. In that same CIO.com roundup, its US enterprise performance leader described ERP as the core system of record, with agentic AI as an outer layer of systems of action, and was direct about the order: on a legacy system, you modernize the core before you layer AI on top. In that view AI doesn't replace ERP. ERP is what makes the AI worth anything.

That's the piece I keep coming back to across all four of these essays. The AI layer is the amplifier. The tracks, the data, the workflow, the operating model underneath it, are still the point, whether you're managing a trillion dollars or running a single ERP system for a business nobody outside your industry has heard of.
